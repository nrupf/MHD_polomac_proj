
\chapter{Relevant Parameters for the Polomac}
\label{chap:parameters}

For the following three dimensionless (or characteristic) parameters governing the MHD
dynamics of the Polomac we first introduce the underlying
physics, then evaluate the parameter using design values
from~\cite{Elio2024}, and finally state which assumption of subsection \ref{subsec:mhd_assumptions} it justifies.

Throughout this chapter we use the following Polomac
values (\cite{Elio2024}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hydrogen plasma: $m_i = \SI{1.67e-27}{\kilogram}$, $Z = 1$
    \item Plasma density: $n = \SI{1e20}{\per\cubic\metre}$
    \item Temperature: $T_i \approx T_e = \SI{100}{\electronvolt}$
    \item Magnetic field: $B = \SIrange{0.2}{0.3}{\tesla}$
    \item Characteristic length: $L \sim \SIrange{0.04}{0.3}{\metre}$
\end{itemize}
Note that some of these values ($n, T, (B)$) are target values and not yet experimentally verified.

\section{Alfv\'en Velocity and Waves}
\label{sec:alfven}

\subsection{Theory of Alfv\'en waves}
\label{subsec: theory of alfven waves}

In this subsection we follow chapter 8 of \cite{Freidberg2014}.

Alfv\'en waves are transverse, incompressible oscillations that
propagate along magnetic field lines, driven by magnetic tension and
resisted by plasma inertia.  To derive their dispersion relation we
linearize the ideal MHD equations around a static uniform equilibrium
$\vec{B}=B_0\hat{e}_z$, $\varrho=\varrho_0$, $\vec{V}=0$,
$P=P_0$, writing each quantity as its equilibrium value plus a small
perturbation (e.g.\ $\vec{B}=B_0\hat{e}_z+\vec{B}_1$).  To first
order, combining the ideal MHD Ohm's law \eqref{eq: MHD ohm's law} 
and Faraday's law \eqref{eq: MHD faraday}, we get the so called induction equation
\begin{align}
    \label{eq: alfven ind. eq}
    \frac{\partial\vec{B}_1}{\partial t}
        &= \nabla\wedge(\vec{V}_1\wedge\vec{B}_0), 
\end{align}

and the ideal MHD momentum equation \eqref{eq: MHD momentum eq.}
combined with the ideal MHD Ampère-Maxwell law \eqref{eq: MHD ampere-maxwell} gives the 

\begin{align}
    \label{eq: alfven mom. eq}
    \varrho_0\frac{\partial\vec{V}_1}{\partial t}
        &= -\nabla P_1
          +\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\nabla\wedge\vec{B}_1)\wedge\vec{B}_0.
\end{align}


We seek plane-wave solutions $Q_1\propto e^{i(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}-\omega t)}$, thus without loss of generality
placing $\vec{k}=k_\perp\hat{e}_y+k_\parallel\hat{e}_z$.
The shear Alfv\'en branch is polarised perpendicular to both $\vec{B}_0$ and
$\vec{k}$, i.e.\ $\vec{V}_1 = V_1\hat{e}_x$.  Because $\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_1=0$
the motion is incompressible and $P_1=0$, so the pressure gradient in the momentum equation \eqref{eq: alfven mom. eq} drops out.

Substituting the plane-wave ansatz into a fourier transformed induction equation
($\partial_t\to -i\omega$, $\nabla\to i\vec{k}$) gives
%
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{B}_1
    = -\frac{1}{\omega}\,\vec{k}\wedge(\vec{V}_1\wedge\vec{B}_0).
\end{equation*}
%
With $\vec{V}_1 = V_1\hat{e}_x$ and $\vec{B}_0=B_0\hat{e}_z$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{V}_1\wedge\vec{B}_0 = -V_1 B_0\,\hat{e}_y
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \vec{B}_1
    = -\frac{k_\parallel B_0}{\omega}\,V_1\,\hat{e}_x.
\end{equation*}
%
Inserting $\vec{B}_1$ into the $\hat{e}_x$-component of the fourier transformed momentum equation:
%
\begin{equation*}
    -i\omega\varrho_0 V_1
    = \frac{i}{\mu_0}
      \bigl[(\vec{k}\wedge\vec{B}_1)\wedge\vec{B}_0\bigr]_x
    = \frac{i}{\mu_0}\,k_\parallel B_0\,B_{1x}
    = -\frac{i k_\parallel^2 B_0^2}{\mu_0\omega}\,V_1.
\end{equation*}
%
Dividing through by $-i\varrho_0 V_1/\omega$ yields the dispersion relation
%
\begin{equation}
    \omega^2 = k_\parallel^2\,v_A^2,
    \label{eq:alfven_disp}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
    v_A = \frac{B_0}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varrho_0}}
    \label{eq:va}
\end{equation}
%
is the \emph{Alfv\'en velocity}.  The relation~\eqref{eq:alfven_disp} is
non-dispersive: phase and group velocities are equal and directed along
$\vec{B}_0$.  Only the parallel wavenumber $k_\parallel$ enters, so the wave
is insensitive to $k_\perp$.  The perturbation involves no compression,
no density fluctuation, and no pressure fluctuation; it is a pure
field-line-bending mode, oscillating between perpendicular kinetic energy
and magnetic tension energy.


\subsection{Alfv\'en velocity of the Polomac}
\label{subsec: alfven velocity of polomac}

With $B = \SI{0.25}{T}$ (midpoint of operating range) and
$\varrho = nm_i = \SI{e20}{m^{-3}}\times\SI{1.67e-27}{kg}
= \SI{1.67e-7}{kg.m^{-3}}$ we get for \eqref{eq:va}:
\begin{equation*}
    v_A
    \approx \SI{5.5e5}{m.s^{-1}}.
\end{equation*}


The corresponding MHD timescale for $L = \SI{0.3}{m}$ is
$\tau_A = L/v_A \approx \SI{5.5e-7}{s}$.



\subsection*{Justification of neglection of displacement current in MHD assumption 4}


\[
    \frac{v_A}{c}
    = \frac{5.5\times10^5}{3\times10^8}
    \approx 1.8\times10^{-3} \ll 1. \quad
\]
Thus the MHD dynamics are non-relativistic and the displacement current in
Amp\`ere's law is negligible.



\section{Lundquist Number}
\label{sec:lundquist}

\subsection*{Physics}


The Lundquist number (see \cite{goldston1995}) compares the resistive diffusion timescale
$\tau_R = \mu_0 L^2/\eta$ (from $\frac{B}{\tau_R} \sim \frac{\eta}{\mu_0} \frac{B}{L^2}$)
 to the Alfv\'en timescale $\tau_A=L/v_A$:
\begin{equation}
    S \coloneqq \frac{\tau_R}{\tau_A} = \frac{\mu_0 L v_A}{\eta}.
    \label{eq:lundquist}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $S$ is the magnetic Reynolds number evaluated at the
Alfv\'en speed.  When $S\gg1$, resistive diffusion is slow on MHD
timescales and the magnetic flux is effectively frozen into the
fluid, the ideal MHD limit.

\subsection{Lundquist number of the Polomac}

Using $\eta\sim\SI{1e-6}{\ohm\metre}$ (Section~\ref{subsec:mhd_assumptions}),
$v_A=\SI{5.5e5}{\metre\per\second}$, and $L=\SIrange{0.04}{0.3}{\metre}$
\[
    S \approx \numrange{2.8e4}{2.1e5}.
\]


\subsection*{Justification of zero resistivity in ideal MHD assumptions}

$S \in [\num{2.8e4},\, \num{2.1e5}]$ means the resistive diffusion
timescale $\tau_R$ exceeds the Alfv\'en timescale $\tau_A$ by four to
five orders of magnitude. Magnetic flux is therefore effectively frozen
into the plasma on MHD timescales, justifying $\eta\to 0$.


\section{Plasma \texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{Beta}}
\label{sec:beta}

\subsection{Physics}

The plasma $\beta$ is the ratio of kinetic plasma pressure to magnetic
pressure and measures confinement efficiency. A higher $\beta$ means more fusion
power per unit of magnetic field energy invested. 
There is no universal definition of plasma $\beta$, the description found in \cite{Freidberg2014}
is for example very much taylored to tokamaks.
Our ansatz, measuring the poloidal $\beta$ because that's all we have in a Polomac, is
\begin{equation}
    \beta \coloneqq \frac{P}{B^2/(2\mu_0)}.
    \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Note that different existing geometries and thus definitions complicate the comparisons of $\beta$.
However, the dominating fields usually also dominate (lower the total value) $\beta$ \cite{Freidberg2014},
so comparisons of efficiency still make sense.

ITER targets a toroidal beta of $\beta_t \approx 0.025$, calculated via 
$\beta_t(\%) = \beta_N \cdot \frac{I_p}{aB_0}$ (\cite{Freidberg2014})
with the baseline normalized beta $\beta_N = 1.8$ (\cite{sips2018}).






\subsection{Plasma \texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{Beta} of the Polomac}
However, since we do not get explicit values for $P$ from the ideal Grad-Shafranov
equation, we will use the parameter ranges given in \cite{Elio2024} (Table~1), where plasma pressure is
\SIrange{1.6}{16}{\kilo\pascal} and magnetic flux density is \SIrange{0.2}{0.3}{\tesla}

Inserting in \eqref{eq:beta}, we thus get 
\begin{equation*}
    \beta \in [0.04, 1.01]
\end{equation*}


\subsection*{Significance for future fusion devices}
If the upper range $\beta\to1$ is achievable, the Polomac could confine fusion-grade
pressures with a comparatively modest magnetic field, reducing both
engineering complexity and power consumption.  These are design targets
and have not yet been experimentally demonstrated.
