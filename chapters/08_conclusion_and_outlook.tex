\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec: conclusion}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

This work developed a self-contained theoretical MHD framework for the Polomac concept,
proceeding from first principles to a concrete numerical implementation plan.

\paragraph{Ideal MHD Equations (Chapter~\ref{chap: Single Fluid Ideal MHD Equations})}
Starting from the Boltzmann equation, velocity moments were taken to obtain species-fluid
equations, which were then combined into a single-fluid model.
The ideal MHD limit was obtained by imposing six assumptions (quasi-neutrality, negligible
electron inertia, negligible displacement current, zero resistivity, small Larmor radius,
and adiabatic isotropic closure), each verified quantitatively for Polomac parameters.
The resulting system consists of a mass-continuity equation, a momentum equation, the
ideal Ohm's law, the adiabatic closure, and the two relevant Maxwell equations.

\paragraph{Polomac Geometry (Chapter~\ref{chap: Polomac geometry and specialization to poloidal configurations}).}
The magnetic field is predominantly poloidal, the toroidal component satisfies
$B_\varphi/|\vec{B}_\mathrm{pol}|\equiv\varepsilon\ll 1$ and enters the MHD equations
only as an $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)$ correction.
To leading order $B_\varphi\equiv 0$, a simplification used throughout all subsequent
chapters.
Axisymmetry is exact in the arc sections and broken by the four magnetic tunnels
in the midplane.

\paragraph{Static Equilibrium (Chapter~\ref{chap: Static equilibrium model in cylindrical coordinates under axisymmetric assumptions}).}
Setting $\vec{V}=0$, $B_\varphi=0$, and $\partial/\partial\varphi=0$ reduces the
force-balance problem to the poloidal flux function $\psi(r,z)$ governed by the
reduced Grad–Shafranov equation \eqref{eq: reduced grad-shafranov eq}.
Boundary conditions are regularity ($\nabla\psi\to 0$ at $r=0$) and
$\psi=\mathrm{const}$ on the conducting walls.
The pressure profile $P(\psi)$ is not determined by ideal MHD and must be supplied by
transport theory or experimental data.

\paragraph{Characteristic Parameters (Chapter~\ref{chap:parameters}).}
Three dimensionless parameters govern the MHD dynamics of the Polomac:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Alfvén velocity.}
        $v_A \approx \SI{5.5e5}{\meter\per\second}$, giving
        $v_A/c\approx\num{1.8e-3}\ll 1$, which justifies neglecting the
        displacement current.
  \item \textbf{Lundquist number.}
        $S\in[\num{2.8e4}, \num{2.1e5}]\gg 1$, confirming that resistive
        diffusion is negligible on MHD timescales and the ideal limit $\eta\to 0$
        is appropriate.
  \item \textbf{Plasma \texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{Beta}.}
        $\beta\in[0.04,\,1.01]$ if the upper range is achievable, the Polomac
        could confine fusion-grade pressures with a modest magnetic field.
        These are design targets and have not been experimentally verified.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Geometry Comparison (Chapter~\ref{chap: An Overview over Magnetic Confinement Approaches for Nuclear Fusion}).}
The Polomac drives no toroidal plasma current, so current-driven kink and tearing
instabilities—and the associated risk of major disruptions—have no analogue here,
unlike in the tokamak.
Steady-state operation is intrinsic to the concept, as in the stellarator.
The principal MHD stability threat is the interchange instability: with identically zero
rotational transform there is no magnetic shear or average good curvature to provide
stabilization, and the only stabilizing mechanism is plasma compressibility,
as demonstrated experimentally in the LDX levitated-dipole experiment
\cite{Garnier2006}.
A Polomac-specific risk is the hot-electron interchange instability: depending on how well the four magnetic
tunnels breaking toroidal axisymmetrywork, they may act analogously to the rod supports that
triggered HEI bursts in the non-levitated LDX operating mode.
Whether compressibility stabilization survives this four-fold symmetry breaking has not
yet been quantitatively assessed \cite{Elio2024}.

\paragraph{FDTD Framework (Chapter~\ref{chap: Framework for a Minimal FDTD Solver for Linearized MHD of the Polomac}).}
The ideal MHD equations were linearized around a uniform static equilibrium
$\vec{B}^{(0)}=B^{(0)}\hat{e}_z$, reduced to 2D axisymmetric cylindrical coordinates,
and shown to form a first-order hyperbolic system with characteristic speeds
$0, v_A, c_s, v_f\}$, where $v_f=\sqrt{v_A^2+c_s^2}$ is the fast
magnetosonic speed (\cite{Freidberg2014}).
A staggered-grid (Yee-type) discretization was derived, separating a decoupled
shear-Alfvén branch $(V_\varphi,B^{(1)}_\varphi)$ from the compressional branch
$(V_r,V_z,B^{(1)}_r,B^{(1)}_z,P^{(1)})$.
The CFL stability bound is $\Delta t\leq\Delta x/(\sqrt{2}\,v_f)$, requiring
$\sim\mathcal{O}(10^3-10^4)$ timesteps per Alfvén time for a 100-point grid at Polomac
parameters.

\subsection{Numerical Implementation}
\label{subsec: numerical implementation}

The immediate next step is to implement the FDTD solver specified in
Chapter~\ref{chap: Framework for a Minimal FDTD Solver for Linearized MHD of the Polomac}.
Correctness should be established through the three verification tests described in
Section~\ref{subsec: verification tests}:
(i) a plane-wave test confirming that the shear-Alfvén and fast-magnetosonic dispersion
relations from \autoref{subsec: types of PDEs} are
reproduced to second order globally in $\Delta r$, $\Delta z$, $\Delta t$;
(ii) conservation of the discrete total energy over many Alfvén times; and
(iii) machine-precision preservation of the discrete $\nabla\cdot\vec{B}^{(1)}=0$
constraint \eqref{eq: gauss magnetism constraint}.

\subsection{Equilibrium beyond the Uniform Background}
\label{subsec: equilibrium beyond uniform}

The FDTD framework currently linearizes around a uniform $B^{(0)}\hat{e}_z$
equilibrium.
A natural and physically more relevant next step is to use a Grad–Shafranov
solution fulfilling \eqref{eq: reduced grad-shafranov eq} with an experimentally
verified or from transport theory derived pressure profile $P(\psi)$ as the equilibrium state
and linearize around it.

\subsection{Extension to Non-Axisymmetric Perturbations}
\label{subsec: non-axisymmetric perturbations}

The solver framework assumes axisymmetry ($\partial/\partial\varphi=0$), corresponding
to the $m=0$ toroidal mode.
Since the equilibrium is axisymmetric, perturbations of arbitrary toroidal mode number
$m$ can be incorporated without increasing the dimensionality of the numerical problem.
Writing every perturbation field as
\begin{equation}
    Q_1(r,\varphi,z,t) = \hat{Q}_1(r,z,t)\,e^{im\varphi}, \qquad m\in\mathbb{Z},
    \label{eq: toroidal mode ansatz}
\end{equation}
replaces $\partial/\partial\varphi\to im$ everywhere in the linearized equations
\eqref{eq: lin continuity}-\eqref{eq: lin pressure}. Equation~\eqref{eq: toroidal mode ansatz} is inspired by the Ansatz for the
$\theta$-pinch from section 11.3 of \cite{Freidberg2014}.
Each mode number $m$ then yields an independent 2D problem on the same $(r,z)$ grid,
with additional $im/r$ terms appearing in the divergence and curl operators.
The solver structure, CFL condition, and boundary conditions should carry over with only minor
modifications; the one qualitative change is that $V_\varphi$ and $B^{(1)}_\varphi$
no longer decouple from the poloidal components for $m\neq 0$, so all six field
components must be evolved simultaneously.
At the axis $r=0$ the regularity condition becomes mode-dependent: fields must vanish
there for $m\geq 1$, because they need to be the same for all $\varphi$.

Surveying the lowest few mode numbers is of direct physical interest given the
closed-field-line geometry discussed in Section~\ref{sec: conclusion}.
The four magnetic tunnels break axisymmetry with four-fold symmetry, possibly coupling $m=4k$
and their harmonics and destabilizing the fields.
\subsection{Resources and Collaboration}
\label{subsec: resources}
A natural starting point for further numerical work is exploring the resources
already established by the Levitating Dipole Experiment (LDX).

The OpenStar project is currently developing a levitated-dipole reactor concept in a
regime closely related to the Polomac. Possibly an exchange of numerical methods and stability
analysis, as well as other collaboration, would be mutually beneficial.
