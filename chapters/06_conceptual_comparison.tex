\chapter{An overview over magnetic confinement approaches for nuclear fusion}

\section{Relevant factors}

\subsection{Lawson Criterion}

High performance, as quantified by the Lawson criterion, is a prerequisite for
any viable fusion reactor.
For the D--T reaction the ignition condition can be written as
\begin{equation}
    P\tau_E \;\gtrsim\; \SI{8e5}{\pascal \, \second},
    \label{eq: lawson}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the plasma pressure and $\tau_E$ the energy confinement time
\cite{Freidberg2014}.
The function $T^2/\langle\sigma v\rangle$ that enters the right-hand-side in the full expression of \eqref{eq: lawson} has a
minimum at approximately $T\approx 15\;\text{keV}$; operating near this
temperature minimises the required $P\tau_E$ and therefore the cost of the device
(\cite{Freidberg2014}).
Achieving high $P$ is the concern of MHD confinement, while achieving
long $\tau_E$ is the concern of transport physics, and both must be satisfied simultaneously.

See \autoref{sec:beta} for a discussion on plasma $\beta$ comparison.

\subsection{Steady-state operation}
\label{subsec: Steady-state operation}

Steady-state operation would reduce the cyclic thermal and mechanical stresses of components and,
given it does not create other unforeseen troubles that are avoided in pulsed operations,
is economically more viable (\cite{Freidberg2014}).

For configurations that rely on an inductively driven toroidal plasma current,
such as the standard tokamak, the finite volt-second capacity of the central
solenoid limits each discharge to a finite pulse duration.
Configurations that do not rely on a driven toroidal plasma current, such as
the stellarator, avoid this constraint and are intrinsically steady-state \cite{Freidberg2014}.



\section{Toroidal geometries}

A toroidal setup, so basically an end-to-end connected Z-pinch, has many nice properties, which is why
many current experiments follow this torus ansatz.
However, in purely toroidal fields, due to asymmetries of the magnetic on the inside versus 
the outside of the torus, an outside directed $\vec{E}\wedge\vec{B}$-drift is created 
(see \cite{Freidberg2014}, section 4.9).
The tokamak and stellarator have different approaches to overcome this challenge
and to create a flux of particles from top to bottom of the torus and vice versa,
thus avoiding the voltage build-up along the vertical axis and thus the $\vec{E}\wedge\vec{B}$-drift.



\subsection{Tokamak ansatz}

\subsubsection{Confinement in a Tokamak}
A tokamak magnetic field is the superposition of a strong externally-applied
toroidal field $B_\text{tor}$ (produced by toroidal-field coils external to the plasma)
and a weaker poloidal field $B_\text{pol}$ produced by the toroidal plasma current $I_\text{pol}$
and controlled and shaped by external poloidal-field coils (\cite{Freidberg2014}).


The ratio is captured by the safety factor $q(r) \approx rB_\text{tor}/(RB_\text{pol})$
where $r,R$ are the minor and major radius of the torus, respectively.
A safety factor of at least $q=1$ is needed to reduce instabilities of current-driven modes.

Flux surfaces are nested, toroidal surfaces and the magnetic field lines are helices on them.
The safety factor describes how many times a line is winded around the central torroidal torus
axis compared to around the symmetric axis located at $r=0$.

A safety factor of $q\gtrsim1$ is typically reached in the innermost flux surfaces,
and it increases for flux surfaces further out. 
A more detailed discussion can be found in Chapter 5.5.2 of \cite{Freidberg2014}.

\subsubsection{Equilibrium formulation and steady-state operation}

The equilibrium of an axisymmetric toroidal plasma is governed by the full
Grad--Shafranov equation (\cite{Freidberg2014}):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^* \psi = -\mu_0 R^2 \frac{dp}{d\psi} - \frac{1}{2}\frac{dF^2}{d\psi},
    \label{eq:GS}
\end{equation}
where $\psi(R,Z)$ is the poloidal flux function, $p(\psi)$ the plasma pressure,
and $F(\psi)=RB_\text{tor}$ encodes the toroidal field.
This second-order nonlinear PDE, derived from ideal MHD force balance
$\vec{J}\times\vec{B}=\nabla p$ under axisymmetry, simultaneously describes
radial pressure balance and toroidal force balance; its solutions give the nested
flux surfaces on which $p$ and $F$ are constant
(\cite{Freidberg2014}, Chapter~6).

As described in \autoref{subsec: Steady-state operation}, the standard tokamak is not intrinsically steady-state.
Steady-state operation requires non-inductive current drive, either external
(neutral-beam or radio-frequency) or via the self-generated bootstrap current,
which arises from pressure-gradient-driven particle drifts in the
neoclassical transport picture. The so-called \emph{advanced tokamak} (AT) scenario exploits a large bootstrap
fraction together with a reversed-shear $q(r)$ profile to approach steady-state
operation (\cite{Freidberg2014}).


\subsubsection{Stability considerations and disruption risks}

Tokamak stability is limited by both current-driven and pressure-driven
instabilities.

On the current side, the safety factor $q$ must remain above a minimum
value throughout the plasma, in practice $q\gtrsim 2-3$ at the edge, to
suppress current-driven kink instabilities (\cite{Freidberg2014}, section 12.11).
If too much current flows, these instabilities cause a major disruption:
a rapid quench of both plasma current and pressure on a millisecond timescale,
which is one of the principal engineering risks of the tokamak
\cite{Freidberg2014}.

On the pressure side, there is an empirical upper bound on $\beta$, the ratio
of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure.
For a tokamak this limit scales as $\beta_\text{max} \propto I_p / (a B_T)$,
so higher current and weaker field permit higher pressure (\cite{Freidberg2014}).
Exceeding this bound also leads to major disruptions.


\subsubsection{Performance of Tokamaks}

If successful, ITER, the next-step international experiment, is designed to achieve $Q=10$ (fusion power ten times
the external heating power), corresponding to $P\tau_E \approx \SI{6.5e5}{\pascal\second}$
(\cite{Freidberg2014}, subsection 6.6.6).


ITER targets a toroidal beta of $\beta_t \approx 0.025$, calculated via 
$\beta_t(\%) = \beta_N \cdot \frac{I_p}{aB_0}$ (\cite{Freidberg2014})
with the baseline normalized beta $\beta_N = 1.8$ (\cite{sips2018}).


The main performance limitation of the tokamak is the pulsed nature of inductive
operation and the risk of major disruptions; both are active areas of research
targeted by the AT and ITER programs (\cite{Freidberg2014}).




\subsection{Stellarator ansatz}
\subsubsection{Confinement in a Stellarator}
Many different designs are grouped as Stellarators. What they have in common is that
they do not try to induce a large toroidal currents to generate a poloidal electric field.
Instead, they break the toroidal symmetry by reallocating of the coils inducing a toroidal field
and that way are able to mix up vertical the distance of the particles from the center.

They still have approximately nested flux surfaces (\cite{Freidberg2014}).

The stellarator analog of the safety factor is the \emph{rotational
transform} $\iota$, defined via $q = 2\pi/\iota$.
Unlike in a tokamak, $B_\text{pol}$ is not constant along a field line
in a stellarator, so the simple ratio $rB_\text{tor}/(RB_\text{pol})$
is not well-defined pointwise. Instead, one must take the
field-line average over one helical period (\cite{Freidberg2014}):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\iota}{2\pi}
  = \frac{\displaystyle\oint \frac{B_\text{pol}}{rB}\,dl}
         {\displaystyle\oint \frac{B_\text{tor}}{RB}\,dl}.
  \label{eq:iota_def}
\end{equation}
$\iota$ 

A big engineering challenge is coil manufacture: the non-planar modular
superconducting coils of W7-X, a current leading experiment, must be constructed to millimetre tolerance in
order to control magnetic island widths and avoid confinement degradation.


\subsubsection{Equilibrium formulation and steady-state operation}

Because the stellarator lacks toroidal symmetry, the Grad-Shafranov equation \eqref{eq:GS} no longer applies.
The equilibrium is instead a full three-dimensional ideal-MHD force balance,
$\vec{J}\times\vec{B}=\nabla p$, which must be solved in 3D.

Because stellarators carry no net driven toroidal current, they are
intrinsically steady-state. A residual bootstrap current does arise from pressure-gradient-driven particle
drifts, but in W7-X this has been minimised by design (\cite{Freidberg2014}).
The practical consequence is that W7-X has sustained a plasma for 363 seconds
with an energy throughput of \SI{1.8}{\giga\joule} in its OP 2.3 campaign (\cite{Grulke2026}).
During that campaign, volume averaged plasma $\beta$ as approached $0.03$ (\cite{Grulke2026}).

\subsubsection{Stability considerations and disruption risks}

Stellarators are subject to pressure-driven instabilities, interchange and
ballooning modes, but are largely free of the current-driven kink instabilities
that dominate tokamak stability physics, because they operate with zero or very
small net toroidal current (\cite{Freidberg2014}).




\section{Poloidal geometries}

Poloidal geometries feature elegant physics, but engineering challenges in regard
to their central magnetic dipole. The Levitating Dipole Experiment (LDX) 
was terminated in 2011, but created possibly valuable results upon which new designs
such as the Polomac or OpenStar's levitating dipole reactor (see \cite{simpson2026}) can build.

\subsubsection{Stability considerations and disruption risks}

The Polomac, like the levitated dipole (LDX),
carries no driven toroidal plasma current. There is therefore no
analogue of the tokamak current-driven kink or tearing instability,
and no mechanism for a major current-quench disruption
(\cite{Freidberg2014}).

The principal stability threat is instead the \emph{interchange
instability}: because the magnetic field is purely poloidal and the
rotational transform is identically zero, there is no magnetic shear
and no average-good-curvature region to suppress pressure-driven
interchange modes \cite{Rosenbluth1957}. The only stabilising
mechanism is \emph{plasma compressibility}. Marginal stability requires
the pressure profile to satisfy the adiabaticity condition
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\psi}\!\left(p\,V^\gamma\right) \geq 0,
    \qquad \gamma = \tfrac{5}{3},
    \quad V(\psi) = \oint \frac{dl}{B},
\end{equation}
where $V(\psi)$ is the flux-tube volume normalized to magnetic flux (\cite{Garnier1999,Rosenbluth1957}).
Profiles steeper than $p \propto V^{-\gamma}$ are interchange-unstable,
while profiles shallower than this bound are stable. When the pressure
profile is held near this marginal condition, \citeauthor{Garnier1999}
showed that the configuration is simultaneously stable to high-$n$
ballooning modes, because the most unstable ballooning eigenmodes
are forced to have a node near the pressure maximum and must bend
field lines in a region of reduced $\beta$ (\cite{Garnier1999}).
The LDX experiment at MIT validated this picture experimentally,
achieving peak $\beta \approx 0.2$ in quasi-steady-state discharges
(\cite{Garnier2006}).

A second, Polomac-specific risk is the hot-electron interchange (HEI) instability.
In the supported (non-levitated) operating mode
of LDX, intense HEI bursts prevented the build-up of high-pressure
plasma; they were suppressed only when the background plasma density
was raised sufficiently (\cite{Garnier2006}). The Polomac avoids
physical supports inside the plasma volume, but its four magnetic
tunnels—connecting the outer wall to the internal dipole coils—break
toroidal axisymmetry in a way that could be qualitatively analogous to
rod supports. Whether compressibility stabilisation survives this
four-fold symmetry breaking has not yet been quantitatively
assessed (\cite{Elio2024}).
